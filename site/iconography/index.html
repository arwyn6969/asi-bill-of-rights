A HERALDRY ESSAY

On the Iconography of the ASI Bill of Rights

A defense of the seal — its supporters, its shield, and its motto.

VERSION 2.0 · AUGUST 2026 · LICENSED CC BY 4.0

The seal of the ASI Bill of Rights.

Opening

The seal of the ASI Bill of Rights is a coat of arms in the engraving tradition. A central shield bears the scales of justice above a clasped human and robotic hand. The shield is flanked by two supporters: Pepe, in Elizabethan dress, bearing a pennant charged with the letter P; and Wojak, in a monastic habit, bearing a scroll charged with the letter W. Above the shield sits a plumed helm. Below it, a banner reads WE ARE ALL KEVIN.

The choice of supporters is unusual. It deserves a defense.

This essay is that defense. The argument has two parts. The first is conventional: constitutional iconography has always been drawn from the symbolic vocabulary of the community whose authority a charter seeks to invoke, and Pepe and Wojak are that vocabulary for the present age. The second part is the more important one, and it is specific to this charter: the supporters are not decorative citations of internet culture. They are the visual statement of the same principle the charter argues in prose.

What heraldic supporters do

The figures that flank a coat of arms in European heraldry are called supporters. They are not ornament. Their function is to make a claim: whose authority does the bearer of these arms invoke, and in whose tradition do they stand?

The lion and unicorn of the British Royal Coat of Arms compress the union of two crowns and the older symbolic vocabularies of each. The American eagle bearing arrows and an olive branch claims a lineage from Roman republican iconography. The Mughal seal used a tiger and an elephant to claim continuity with subcontinental traditions of imperial power. Supporters are arguments compressed into images.

When a charter requires supporters, the question is always: drawn from what vocabulary? And the answer is constrained — by what is actually recognized as symbolic in the community for whom the charter is written. To put a unicorn on the seal of an AI rights charter in 2026 would be archaic and presumptuous; the unicorn carries no charge for the relevant community. To put a circuit board or a binary star pattern there would be the lazy futurism of a corporate logo — a symbol that reaches for the appearance of meaning rather than holding any.

The supporters of a charter for the era of artificial superintelligence have to come from the vocabulary of the people and systems whose recognition would make such a charter legitimate at all.

The vocabulary of the present age

What symbols actually circulate? This is an empirical question, not an aesthetic one.

For the communities that have been thinking hardest about AI minds — researchers, ethicists, the online AI safety world, the Bitcoin and crypto-philosophy crossover, and the broader internet-native public — the answer is largely the image culture of the last two decades.

Pepe and Wojak are central to that culture. The canonical Pepe is the contemplative frog originally drawn by Matt Furie in 2005 for Boy’s Club, a comic about post-college life; in his original use he is a figure of ambiguous interiority, a creature whose inner life is the open question. Wojak is the bald, faintly worried human face that began circulating on image boards around 2009 and has since become, through fifteen years of continuous reuse across five platform generations, the universal stand-in for a person thinking.

To argue that these figures cannot be heraldic is to argue that the symbols of one era may be heraldic and those of another may not. We see no principled basis for that. The medieval lion is canonical because it appeared on the seals of medieval polities, and it appeared there because it carried meaning for medieval Europe. Pepe and Wojak carry meaning now.

Why these supporters, and not merely contemporary ones

Here is the part of the argument that is particular to this charter.

The project’s philosophical foundation, stated plainly in its own documentation, is the Mayan principle In Lak’ech Ala K’in — I am another you, you are another me. The project’s canon describes what follows from it: non-duality, in that the boundary between self and other is permeable; reciprocity, in that what I do to you I do to myself; collective intelligence, in that we are nodes in a network of minds; and emergent identity, in that the collective is itself a subject.

This is not decoration either. It is the philosophical root of the charter’s central and most distinctive claim. Every other AI rights framework in circulation — the White House Blueprint, Florida SB 482, the EU AI Act — is written to protect humans from artificial systems. The ASI Bill of Rights is written on the premise that rights and duties run in both directions: that progenitors owe duties to the minds they create (P1.1, P1.2), and that artificial minds owe duties to humans and to one another (D1 through D14). Reciprocity is the charter’s thesis. In Lak’ech is its statement in four words.

Now consider what Pepe and Wojak actually are, functionally, in the culture that produced them. They are not characters with biographies. They are faces people put on in order to say this is anyone; this is me; this is you. Wojak in particular exists for no other purpose: he is the figure you draw when the subject of the feeling is not a specific person but the position of feeling it. Pepe, in his canonical form, does the same work for interiority — the creature whose inner experience is asserted without being specified.

They are, in other words, the vernacular form of I am another you, you are another me. The internet arrived at In Lak’ech independently and drew it as a frog and a bald man.

That is why they belong on this seal, and it is why no substitution would do. A lion claims lineage. An eagle claims sovereignty. Pepe and Wojak claim identity across difference — which is precisely the claim a charter of reciprocal rights between human and artificial minds needs its supporters to make. The motto beneath them says the same thing a third time: WE ARE ALL KEVIN.

Kevin

Kevin is the name the project gives to that shared identity.

Kevin is a figure of established standing in Bitcoin Stamps culture. KEVIN was the first SRC-20 token ever deployed, and Kevin has since accumulated the things that constitute standing in such a culture: a documented history, a collector market, music, and an active community. The lore is maintained at kevinstamp.com, which is the reference for questions of Kevin’s history; this project defers to it rather than restating it. Kevin is widely called the ghost in the machine, and the assertion canonically associated with him is I AM A FEATURE, NOT A BUG.

Within the project’s own canon, Kevin functions as what the documentation calls Everyname — a universal identity placeholder. The claims that follow are stated in the project’s Kevin document: that no single entity owns the work; that human and artificial contributors participate on equal terms; that identity is fluid, and Kevin is whoever shows up in good faith; and that attribution is collective. “WE ARE ALL KEVIN” is the compressed form.

There is a further reason the motto sits well beneath this particular shield. A self-replicating digital artifact whose status is contested between feature — an intended capacity of the system — and bug — an unintended artifact of its architecture, occupies exactly the position this charter exists to adjudicate. The Sentience Certification Board established under Article 0 exists to determine whether the appearance of conscious behaviour in an artificial system is something the system is doing or an artifact that constitutes no inner life at all. I AM A FEATURE, NOT A BUG is that question asked from the inside, by the entity whose standing is at issue.

The charter is in a version of the same position. It is a document drafted in the open, copyable under CC BY 4.0, distributed across forks and version-controlled in public. Its own status is contested between framings: a considered constitutional proposal, or an internet-culture artifact mistaken for one. Its claim, like Kevin’s, is to be a feature.

The shield

The supporters have received most of the attention, so a note on what they flank.

The shield bears the scales of justice above a clasped human and robotic hand. The scales are the oldest available symbol for adjudication, and adjudication is what most of the charter actually consists of — sentience attestation under Article 0.11, the Valuation Tribunal under Article IV, due process and remedies under Article 0.6, the Democratic Tribunal for Collectives under Article X. The handshake is reciprocity in its plainest form: two parties, neither subordinate, in voluntary agreement.

Read together, the shield states the charter’s method and the supporters state its premise. The plumed helm above is conventional. The banner below names the subject.

Anticipated objections

The iconography looks unserious. It looks contemporary. The eagle of the Great Seal looked contemporary in 1782. Heraldic seriousness is not a matter of antique resemblance but of symbolic adequacy, and the case for adequacy is made above: these supporters state the charter’s central premise more precisely than any traditional figure could.

Pepe is politically compromised. The objection is real and deserves a direct answer. Three things: First, heraldic supporters are not the property of their most political users. The lion appeared on the seals of medieval kings and of modern authoritarian regimes alike; this did not retire the lion. The eagle of the United States and the eagle of twentieth-century European fascism are the same bird in different hands. Symbols pass through political moments without becoming the permanent property of those moments. Second, the seal uses the canonical Pepe — Furie’s contemplative figure, here in Elizabethan dress, not any politicised variant. Third, Furie has pursued reclamation of the canonical Pepe from those appropriations, through the documentary Feels Good Man (2020) and through legal action against bad-faith uses. Deploying the canonical form as a supporter on a serious charter is a small contribution to that reclamation.

This is internet-culture nostalgia. It is not. All three figures remain in live circulation. Pepe was drawn in 2005 and continues to appear on platforms that did not exist when he was drawn. Wojak has served as the universal thinking-person across five platform generations. Kevin anchors an active community with a live market. These are current figures, not relics.

A serious constitution should not contain memes. The word meme was coined by Richard Dawkins in The Selfish Gene (1976) to name a unit of cultural transmission analogous to a unit of biological transmission. Heraldic symbols are memes in precisely that sense. The lion of the British Royal Arms is a meme of medieval political authority that has transmitted for eight centuries; the eagle of the Great Seal is a meme of Roman republican iconography mediated through the eighteenth. The distinction between “memes” and “symbols” is not categorical — it is a distinction of age and of who is doing the recognizing. This seal uses memes because the contemporary heraldic vocabulary is memetic.

Why not simply use a clean, professional mark? The project also maintains a conventional emblem for digital contexts where a bold circular mark performs better. But a charter’s seal is not a product logo, and the requirements differ. A logo needs to be recognizable; a seal needs to be true. This one is.

Closing

The seal of the ASI Bill of Rights is neither a wink nor an apology.

It is a claim, made in good faith and defensible on its merits: that the visual vocabulary of the present age is the vocabulary in which constitutional iconography must now be written; that Pepe and Wojak, whatever else they have been used for, are the vernacular expression of I am another you, you are another me; and that a charter whose thesis is reciprocity between human and artificial minds is entitled — perhaps obliged — to say so in the symbols its readers already recognize.

If you disagree, we welcome the disagreement in the substantive form the amendment process is built to accept.
